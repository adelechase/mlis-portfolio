Leadership & Management

Students will be able to perform effectively in the library and information professions by demonstrating competency in leadership and management. By the conclusion of the master's degree program, students should be able to:

  1. identify and define goals; develop measureable and single-outcome objectives for each goal and define effective strategies for achieving objectives as part of the strategic planning process

  2. design and conduct community analysis, needs assessment, and/or outcome evaluation

  3. develop appropriate content for funding proposals

  4. explain supervisory styles and organizational/management structures

  5. explain strategies for successful financial management, public relations, and marketing

  6. explain and design effective strategies for community and institutional advocacy

  7. promote the role of information services in facilitating diversity and global awareness

  8. analyze ethical issues and develop strategies to address them

Description

For this assignment, my group created an advocacy plan for the Atlanta University Center (AUC) Robert W. Woodruff Library, with a specific focus on services for Spelman College students. Our plan, Refocusing the Lens: Expanding Services for Inclusion, centered on advocacy for three student populations: first-generation college students, autistic and neurodivergent students, and LGBTQIA+ students. The purpose of the project was to think about how an academic library can move beyond general support and intentionally advocate for specific communities within its user population. Since Spelman is a historically Black women’s college, we wanted to honor that institutional context while also recognizing that even within a strong and supportive campus community, some students may still have needs that are less visible or less fully addressed.

Analysis

This project showed our ability to use community analysis, needs assessment, and evaluation as the foundation for library planning. We did this by looking closely at the Spelman and AUC context, identifying specific student communities whose needs could be better supported, and building assessment into the plan through surveys, collection review, usage statistics, attendance tracking, and other measures of engagement. The plan also demonstrated our ability to create advocacy strategies at both the community and institutional level. Instead of only saying that the library should be more inclusive, we designed specific services, partnerships, spaces, training, and outreach efforts that would help the Woodruff Library advocate for students in visible and practical ways. Finally, the plan showed how information services can support diversity, equity, and broader awareness by making the library a place where students’ identities, experiences, and research needs are reflected in its programming, collections, and services.

Reflection

An advocacy plan requires a lot of different pieces to work together: community analysis, institutional context, mission and values, proposed services, partnerships, assessment, and a realistic timeline. The group had an easy time coming up with ideas, but it took some time to settle on the ones we would actually use in the plan because it was not enough to simply come up with good program ideas; those ideas had to make sense for the specific library, the specific student population, and the goals of the assignment. We had to think about what advocacy looks like when it is built into library services in a practical way. Similarly, I think libraries sometimes are attracted to shiny new technologies and services without thinking about how exactly they are meant to support patrons. This assignment provided a valuable lesson in the practicalities of connecting theory with practice.
