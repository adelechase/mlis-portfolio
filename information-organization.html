Information & Its Organization

Students will be able to perform effectively in the library and information professions by demonstrating competency in information and its organization. By the conclusion of the master’s degree program, students should be able to:

  1. explain major concepts, theories, issues and trends in knowledge representation and information organization

  2. explain the relationship between information organization and information retrieval

  3. explain information-seeking behavior in theoretical and practical terms

  4. demonstrate proficient professional skills in information searching

  5. explain and demonstrate the techniques of bibliographic organization and control

  6. explain the principles and techniques of physical and digital preservation of materials

Information & Its Organization

Description

For this assignment, I wrote an essay that analyses how information retrieval systems organize, represent, and make information findable. The assignment asked me to choose from several approved IR systems, practice different kinds of searches, examine the metadata available in search results or catalog records, and compare how different systems index information. I chose to evaluate Google Books, the Library of Congress catalog, and the Open Library in order to think through how different systems handle searching, metadata, and access.

Analysis

My essay demonstrates how metadata and indexing methods affect the ability to find relevant information by demonstrating my skills in information retrieval by comparing Google Books and the Library of Congress catalog, showing how full-text indexing, metadata-only searching, controlled vocabulary indexing, and social indexing shape search results and retrieval effectiveness. It required me to practice with multiple IR systems and multiple search types, including simple search, advanced search, keyword search, metadata search, full-text search, subject search, Boolean searching, and authority control. Through the essay’s focus on metadata, cataloging records, authority control, controlled vocabulary searching, subject searching, and indexing methods, I demonstrated the techniques of bibliographic organization and control. By exploring descriptive elements that help users locate materials, I was able to connect organizational structures to actual retrieval outcomes. For example, I compared searching for Treasure Island by Robert Louis Stevenson in Google Books and in the Library of Congress catalog to show how indexing methods affect what users find and how easily they might find it.

Reflection

This assignment was interesting because it required me to think "beyond" the search box. I am comfortable using search tools, but this assignment forced me to slow down and ask myself why exactly different systems return different results. It's not enough to say that Google Books "feels" easier to use than the Library of Congress catalog; I had to think about the specifics — metadata, indexing, controlled vocabularies, authority records, and full-text searching — as the structures that make retrieval possible. The shift from using a system to analyzing how the system works was the most challenging and useful part of the assignment. A search strategy that works well in one place may work awkwardly, incompletely, or not at all somewhere else. That made the assignment a bit frustrating, but it also made the lesson more practical. In real library work, searching is rarely one-size-fits-all. Effective searching requires understanding both the user’s question and the inner workings of the system being searched.
